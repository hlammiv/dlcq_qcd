\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In 1990, Hornbostel, Brodsky and Pauli diagonalized the light-cone Hamiltonian
of SU($N$) gauge theory in one space and one time dimension in a discrete
momentum basis, and computed the hadronic spectrum and wavefunctions across
couplings, numbers of colours, and baryon number~\cite{Hornbostel:1988fb}.
The calculation ran at resolutions $2K = 16$--$24$, in minutes on the
mainframes of the day, with convergence controlled by a single extrapolation
formula.  It became a standard reference for discretized light-cone
quantization (DLCQ), and its figures are still the picture most physicists
carry of what QCD in two dimensions looks like at finite $N$: which states
exist, how their wavefunctions deform with coupling, how quickly large-$N$
becomes a good approximation.

The reasons to study the model have, if anything, strengthened since.
Tractability remains: the theory confines, has a meaningful chiral limit, and
carries one dimensionless parameter, while being solvable to high precision.
The role of test bed has changed hands --- where the 1990 paper looked ahead
to DLCQ in four dimensions, today the same model is the standard benchmark
for Hamiltonian lattice methods, tensor networks, and quantum simulation,
which makes controlled finite-$N$ spectra directly useful to communities that
did not exist then.  And the model has open questions of its own: the
chiral-limit behaviour at finite $N$ is measured differently by light-front
and equal-time methods (Sec.~\ref{sec:modern}), and the existence of compact
multiquark states near threshold is an active large-$N$ prediction awaiting a
finite-$N$ test~\cite{Sazdjian:2025aa,Lucha:2017gqq}.

This paper re-does the 1990 calculation with thirty-six years of hardware and
--- more importantly --- of error analysis.  We reproduce every figure and the
table of that paper, at the original parameters and again at resolutions up
to $2K \simeq 100$, from two independent implementations of the same
Hamiltonian: the preserved Fortran of the original program, and a validated
open-source port.  Where the original controlled its errors with one
extrapolation series and a parenthesis convention, we carry a four-component
error budget with held-out validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}); where its
weak-coupling entries were limited by the momentum grid in a way no
resolution can repair, we identify the mechanism, quantify it without free
parameters, and reach the chiral region instead with van de Sande's improved
Hamiltonian~\cite{vandeSande:1996mc} (Sec.~\ref{sec:chiral}).  Two sections
have no 1990 counterpart: a search for states dominated by four- and
six-parton Fock sectors --- tetraquark and hexaquark candidates ---
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exotics}), and a comparison against the modern record:
integrability results at large $N$, tensor-network and quantum-hardware
spectra at $N = 2, 3$, and precision Schwinger-model benchmarks
(Sec.~\ref{sec:modern}).

We have kept the structure of Ref.~\cite{Hornbostel:1988fb} deliberately,
section for section where possible, so that each figure here can be laid
beside its 1990 counterpart.  Sections~\ref{sec:lcq} and \ref{sec:sun} fix
notation and recall the Hamiltonian; Sec.~\ref{sec:method} describes what
changed computationally; Secs.~\ref{sec:wavefunctions}--\ref{sec:massless}
reproduce and extend the 1990 results; the remainder is new.
