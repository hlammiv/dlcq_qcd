\section{Comparison with modern methods}
\label{sec:modern}

The 1990 paper compared against the two external results then available:
't~Hooft's large-$N$ solution and Hamer's finite-lattice SU(2)
spectrum~\cite{Hamer:1981yq}.  The modern record is far richer.  This
section is organized by what each method can check.

\subsection{Large $N$ and integrability}
\label{sec:modern-largeN}

The 't~Hooft equation is now solved to arbitrary precision: it is
equivalent to a TQ-Baxter
system~\cite{Ambrosino:2023dik,Litvinov:2024riz}, which makes the
large-$N$ meson spectrum exact for our purposes.  We rescale our finite-$N$
masses to 't~Hooft's normalization (Appendix~\ref{app:units}) and find the
expected $1/N^2$ approach, updating Fig.~8(b) of
Ref.~\cite{Hornbostel:1988fb} with error bars.  The same comparison shows
what large $N$ misses at strong coupling: light baryons, and the U(1)
distinction of Sec.~\ref{sec:massless}.  The original argued both points;
here the residuals are quantified.

\subsection{Lattice}
\label{sec:modern-lattice}

Hamer's finite-lattice masses remain the only equal-time spectrum across
the full coupling range at $N = 2$.  We compare against his published
Table~1.  Its stated weak-coupling uncertainties are of order the values
themselves; digitizing his figure would suggest a precision his own table
disclaims, so we do not.  Within those bars, agreement is complete.  That
includes the $N = 2$ identity $M_{\rm bar} = M_{\rm mes}$, which his data
was the first to exhibit.  Modern overlap-fermion work confirms the
expected infrared behavior at $(N_c, N_f) = (2,1)$ and
$(3,1)$~\cite{Karthik:2023gud}, though without spectrum tables to compare
digit for digit.

\subsection{Tensor networks and quantum hardware}
\label{sec:modern-tn}

Matrix-product-state calculations now reach
SU(2)~\cite{Silvi:2016cas,Banuls:2017ena} and
SU(3)~\cite{Silvi:2019wnf,Hayata:2023pkw} in one dimension.  Variational
quantum hardware has produced SU(2) meson and baryon
masses~\cite{Atas:2021ext}, with SU(3) preparations
following~\cite{Farrell:2022wyt}.  These are staggered-fermion computations
at finite lattice spacing, so comparison requires matched continuum
extrapolations, not raw numbers.  Where the published works quote
continuum values, we tabulate them beside ours.  Precision Schwinger-model
benchmarks~\cite{Banuls:2013jaa,Dempsey:2022nys} calibrate what tensor
networks achieve at U(1); our own equal-time exact-diagonalization
cross-checks reproduce them in our conventions
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}).  No public code exists for any of the SU($N$)
tensor-network calculations above.  The pipeline released with this paper
is, to our knowledge, the first reproducible finite-$N$ spectrum code in
either quantization.

\subsection{The chiral-exponent tension}
\label{sec:modern-tension}

One discrepancy in this landscape is structural.  We state it as the open
question it is.  Every light-front determination of the finite-$N$ chiral
exponent finds $M^2 \propto (m/g)^\alpha$ with $\alpha \to 1$: this work,
lightcone conformal truncation~\cite{Anand:2021qnd}, and $1/N$-corrected
't~Hooft perturbation theory~\cite{Kochergin:2024quv}.  The one equal-time
measurement, an MPS study of SU(2) over $m/g \in
[0.1, 0.4]$~\cite{Banuls:2017ena}, reports $\nu = 0.700(29)(11)$ for
$M \propto (m/g)^{\nu}$.  That is $\alpha \simeq 1.4$: consistent with
bosonization, and several standard deviations from the light-front value.

The methods differ in exactly the assumption all light-front schemes share.
The light-front vacuum is trivial, and the vacuum is what an anomalous
chiral exponent is about.  How equal-time vacuum structure re-emerges as
the light cone is approached was analyzed by Hornbostel
himself~\cite{Hornbostel:1991qj}; the question this tension raises is that
analysis made quantitative.  The equal-time side is not yet decisive either.
Its measured window spans a factor of four in $m/g$, not a decade.  And no
SU($N$) analogue of the additive staggered mass
shift~\cite{Dempsey:2022nys} has been published or applied, though the
shift is the same order in the lattice spacing as the mass being fitted.
Resolving the tension requires an equal-time computation in the same chiral
window with that renormalization under control.  It is beyond the published
record, and beyond this paper.
