\section{Convergence and the error budget}
\label{sec:budget}

The 1990 paper controlled its errors with one tool: the extrapolation
series of its Eq.~(27), with the magnitude of the last retained term
printed in parentheses as a convergence diagnostic.  The authors trusted
that diagnostic only for $m/g \gtrsim 0.2$, and said so.  This section
replaces it with a budget of four measured components: fit-form spread,
window spread, Fock-truncation shift, and the endpoint capture deficit.
Definitions and correlations are in Appendix~\ref{app:budget}; every
strategy we measured and rejected is in Appendix~\ref{app:donotretry}.

The first result of doing this is negative, and it shapes the rest.  The
extrapolated mass does not converge as terms are added to the Eq.~(27)
series.  Worse, the two defensible ways to choose the order disagree.
Held-out validation, which fits the low resolutions and predicts the two
highest, picks the largest order offered, every time; it is
near-interpolation, so flexibility always wins.  Consistency with the
known chiral behavior prefers the shortest series.  Two sound criteria
pointing at opposite ends of the range is what an undetermined model order
looks like.  The budget therefore spans the whole ladder rather than
choosing, and that spread is the dominant fit component in every
weak-coupling entry of Table~\ref{tab:tableone}.

Each quoted mass also prints the fraction supplied by extrapolation rather
than data.  At strong coupling the fit interpolates: the extrapolated
fraction is near one percent.  At $m/g = 0.1$ the standard-Hamiltonian
fit supplies over half the answer.  A reader can see, entry by entry,
where the series asserts.

There is a boundary below which no fit on this grid can be trusted, and
two independent criteria put it in the same place.  The endpoint capture
condition $2a \ln K \gtrsim 1$, with $a$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:endpoint-exponent}, fails below $m/g \approx 0.15$ at
$2K = 70$.  A joint fit in $(m/g, K)$ anchored at the chiral point, an
independent method with different systematics, agrees with the
per-coupling fits above that coupling and separates from them below it.
Table~\ref{tab:tableone}'s entries at $m/g = 0.1$ and $0.05$ are
grid-limited in every channel, and $0.2$ is marginal.

The most dangerous property of this failure deserves its own paragraph:
the fits give no warning.  At the smallest coupling we probe, the
standard-Hamiltonian mass series is smooth in $K$, the extrapolation is
well determined, and the fitted curves are identical in shape across a
factor of thirty in coupling.  Nothing visible inside the $K$ convergence
reveals that the answer is three orders of magnitude low.  The arithmetic
says why.  At $m/g = 1.95\times10^{-4}$ a $2K = 70$ grid captures
\numCapturePctTiny\% of the endpoint integral, and halving the remainder,
which falls only as $K^{-a}$, requires a resolution
$10^{\numHalvingLogTen}$ times larger.  No diagnostic that lives inside
the reachable window can see what the window excludes.  That is why the
endpoint deficit enters the budget as a computed systematic
(Appendix~\ref{app:budget}) rather than as something fitted, and why the
chiral region is reached in Sec.~\ref{sec:chiral} by changing the
Hamiltonian rather than the fit.

None of this is a defect of light-cone quantization.  A continuum
't~Hooft solver on a uniform momentum mesh, sharing no code with the DLCQ
path, fails identically (Sec.~\ref{sec:improved}).  The disease is the
uniform grid against a shrinking endpoint region; DLCQ merely inherits it.
